Dr. Joy Buolamwini

About Dr. Buolamwini

Joy Buolamwini was born in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada in 1989 but grew up in the US. Both of her parents were from a Ghanaian background. Her mother was an artist, and her father completed his PhD in pharmaceutical sciences at the University of Alberta. Dr. Joy is currently a computer scientist and researcher known for studying bias in AI. She attended Georgia Institute of Technology, where she earned a degree in computer science.

Joy Buolamwini is considered underrepresented in the technology and AI fields because she is a Black woman working in an industry that has been dominated by white men. She studied computer science and worked on facial recognition systems at MIT, where she discovered that some systems could not recognize her face because of her darker skin tone. She used a white mask to expose this racial bias, and the system instantly recognized her only when she wore it. This moment launched her research into what she calls the "coded gaze."

Her study showed that AI systems made errors less than 1% of the time for lighter-skinned men but had an error rate of over 20 to 34% for darker-skinned women. These findings demonstrated how underrepresentation in training data could cause technology to work poorly for certain groups, creating serious risks of discrimination. Despite facing criticism and resistance from the tech industry, she turned her research into a global movement for fairness in technology.

Why Her Contributions Matter

Most notably, Dr. Buolamwini founded the Algorithmic Justice League, an organization that focuses on fighting and raising awareness for algorithmic bias in AI. This led to people calling her the "conscience of the AI revolution." Through the AJL, she exposed racial and gender bias in AI, set standards for how facial recognition systems should work, influenced government policy to limit facial recognition sales, and argued that AI systems should be harm-tested before being released to the public.

Her Gender Shades project studied the difference in error rates with AI facial recognition between darker-skinned and lighter-skinned individuals. The error rates for darker-skinned individuals were much higher at 34.7%, compared to only 0.8% for lighter-skinned individuals. This project changed the standard for how AI is tested and caused major companies like Microsoft and Amazon to improve their facial analysis software.

Dr. Buolamwini also published the national bestseller Unmasking AI and was the protagonist in the documentary Coded Bias. Her book highlighted that AI reflects its creator's biases, and it helped shift AI policy because her work was cited in court hearings. The documentary pressured companies to re-evaluate their systems, with some even stopping the sale of facial recognition technology to police. Watch a clip here: Coded Bias Trailer.
