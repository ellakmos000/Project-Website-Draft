About Dr. Joy

Background

Joy Buolamwini was born in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada in 1989 but she grew up in the US. Both of her parents were from Ghanaian background. Her mother was an artist, and her father was completing his PhD in pharmaceutical sciences at the University of Alberta. Dr. Joy is currently a computer scientist and researcher known for studying bias in AI. She was interested in technology and computer science from a young age. She attended Georgia Institute of Technology, where she earned a degree in computer science.

Underrepresentation

Joy Buolamwini is considered underrepresented in the technology and artificial intelligence fields because she is a black women working in an industry that has been dominated by white men.She studied computer science and worked on facial recognition systems at MIT and discovered that some facial recognition systems could not recognize her face because of her darker skin tone. She used a white mask to expose racial bias in AI facial recognition systems. The system instantly recognized her if she wore a white mask. These findings demonstrated how underrepresentation in training groups could cause technology to work poorly for certain groups, creating risks of discrimination. Buloawini faced skepticism and resistance from parts of other tech industry. However, she overcame these challenges by turning her research into a global movement for fairness in technology. Her work shows how underrepresented innovators can identify important problems that others overlook and drive change in entire industries.

Contributions

Dr Buolamwini is a decorated woman with countless accomplishments and contributions. Most notably, she founded the Algorithmic Justice League, an organization that focuses on fighting and raising awareness for algorithmic bias in AI which led to people calling her the “conscience of the AI revolution.” . Dr Buolamwini also did extensive research on AI bias such as the “Gender Shades” project and her “Actionable Auditing” study. Her “Gender Shades” project consisted of studying the difference of error rates with AI facial recognition of darker-skinned individuals versus lighter-skinned individuals. As she had anticipated, the error rates for darker-skinned individuals were much higher, 34.7%, compared to the 0.8% error rate for lighter-skinned individuals. The results of this project included introducing the new normal way to test AI by testing across all groups of people, demonstrated the algorithmic bias within AI, and it caused many big industries like Microsoft & Amazon to better their facial analysis software. On the other hand, her “Actionable Auditing” study consisted of investigating the impact of officially naming biased performance results of commercial AI products. This study contributed to raising awareness to and shaming biased performance of AI products, adapted security rules to AI via an audit which forced companies to fix flaws, and it also made people start questioning who was responsible for the biased performance. Not to mention, Dr Buolamwini also published the national bestseller “Unmasking AI,” and she was the protagonist in the documentary “Coded Bias.” Her book, “Unmasking AI,” became a national bestseller instantly for many reasons; It highlighted that we shouldn’t blame and boycott AI for the biased performance because AI isn’t naturally biased, it just reflects its creator’s biases. The book also introduced the term “excised” to refer to people who were wrongly identified by AI, and it helped shift AI policy/regulation because her work was cited in court hearings. As for the documentary “Coded Bias” which she acted as the protagonist in, it was a film that dived into many aspects of algorithmic bias. The film would go on to show that training data consisted of white male faces primarily through experiments and research like the “White Mask” Experiment and Dr Buolamwini’s research called “AI, Ain’t I a Woman?” The research in the film also pressured big industries to reevaluate their systems with some even stopping the sale of facial recognition technology to police. She also challenged the idea that AI is more objective than humans. Of course, just as all of Dr Buolamwini’s work did, the film also raised awareness and shed more light on algorithmic bias

Future Promise

Dr Buolamwini’s goal and purpose of her innovations has always been to eradicate algorithmic biases and build a future for AI and facial recognition systems where they are trained on diverse sets of data. The Algorithmic Justice League will likely continue their focus on making these systems fairer and more accurate even after her death. After Dr Buolamwini’s Gender Shades project, companies immediately fixed their systems to ensure that they’re unbiased such as Amazon. Because of the number of companies that have changed their systems because of her, it is likely that more companies will follow in Amazon’s footsteps to reduce bias in their algorithms. She also talked about the AI Bill of Rights as a step forward helping protections in AI policy. AI systems are going to improve so that facial recognition can detect every person not just a certain group of people.  

Selection

Choosing one underrepresented person for our project was difficult because there were many strong choices. After discussing the options, our group narrowed it down to three innovators. Kimberly Bryant, Fei-Fei Li, and Joy Buolamwini. Kimberly Bryant created Black Girls Code to help girls from underrepresented communities learn coding and technology skills. Fei-Fei Li helped create the ImageNet project, which improved artificial intelligence image recognition. In the end, our group chose Joy Buolamwini because of her research that exposed bias in facial recognition technology. Especially against darker skinned women and pushed major technology companies like Microsoft and Amazon to improve their systems. Her work showed that technology can harm people if it is not tested with different data. Because of this, more companies are starting to test their artificial intelligence more thoroughly, by checking it with many separate groups of data to make sure the data is more diverse and fair.
